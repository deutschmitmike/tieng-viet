== How this app works

You studied lesson 1 from the booklet. Its 62 sentences are already counted as met and come back as reviews over the first five days, in the order of the old booklet days.

Every day there is one round of fifteen minutes; you can change the minutes on the home screen. First the sentences that are due again, then as many new ones as fit. If the reviews pile up, the new sentences wait until they are through.

Nothing plays by itself, and you never have to press Play to go on. Press Play, say the sentence in the pause, press Play again and say it on top of the voice, as often as you like. Then **Again** if it did not come out, **Got it** if it came out fluently, **Easy** if it is so easy that seeing it again soon would waste your time. Again brings the card back a few cards later; Got it sends it further into the future each time.

Once you have pressed Got it for a sentence three times (twice for the lesson 1 sentences, which start one step in), it comes as its meaning only. Say it in Vietnamese, out loud, then press Check and compare with the voice.

The rows of single words from the booklet, the sound drills, are not practised in the app, except the numbers one to ten. They stay in the sentence list and on these pages, to listen to whenever you like.

Slow plays the sentence at three quarters of the speed, same pitch. A dotted syllable means the voice gets that sound wrong; tap it to see what you say instead.

After the round comes the tandem task. There is no check-in: the app notices which sentences are hard, and the next lessons bring them back. Under Practise you can go through any lesson freely; that never disturbs the plan, and Again there brings a sentence into tomorrow's round.


== The five Saigon tones

Vietnamese spelling shows six tones. Saigon has five, because hỏi (ả) and ngã (ã) are pronounced the same. Your Mandarin gives you the categories; not all of the contours are the Mandarin ones.

| tone | mark | Saigon contour | Mandarin comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| ngang | none | mid, flat | like tone 1 but lower, 33 rather than 55 |
| huyền | à | low, falling gently, relaxed | not tone 4: it starts low and falls softly, no force |
| sắc | á | rising | tone 2, nearly identical; on syllables ending in p, t, c, ch it is short and high |
| hỏi / ngã | ả, ã | dips, then rises, longer than the others | a full tone 3 (214), the one you use in isolation |
| nặng | ạ | low, falling, a slight rise at the end, no catch in the throat; short only on syllables ending in p, t, c, ch | the half third tone, like 好 in 好吃 |

Listen for how low nặng sits, how long hỏi is, and that huyền has no energy in it.

### Pronouns

anh = I (a man, to someone younger) and also you (when they address you). em = you (to someone younger) and I (when they speak). Your tandems call you anh and themselves em; you do the reverse. tôi is the neutral I; tui, mình, bạn and the rest come in lesson 4.

### Greetings

Chào em is said quickly, as one unit. Dạ is not "yes" in the German sense; it shows that you are listening politely, and it starts almost every answer to someone you want to be polite to. It is mostly the younger person's word: as anh you will hear it from your tandems more often than you say it.

== Vowels

Twelve vowel letters (i and y share a sound), six of them with a hook or hat that changes the sound entirely. German gives you most of the qualities; the danger is length. Vietnamese a is long, ă and â are short, and length is not decorative: mắt, mất, mát are three words.

| letters | sound | nearest German or Mandarin sound |
|---|---|---|
| a | long, open | Bahn |
| ă | same quality, very short, clipped | the a in "hat", cut even shorter |
| â | short, central, mouth half closed | the e in "bitte", or Mandarin 恩 without the n |
| e | open | Bett |
| ê | closed | Beet |
| i, y | same sound, spelling only | Mandarin 一 |
| o | open, rounded | offen |
| ô | closed, rounded | Ofen, Mandarin 喔 |
| ơ | unrounded ô, mouth relaxed, long | close to the e of Mandarin 餓; â is its short partner |
| u | closed, rounded | Mandarin 烏 |
| ư | u with the lips spread as for i | no clean match; start from u and unround |

### Glides

ia, ua, ưa at the end of a syllable are i, u, ư gliding into a lazy ơ. Before a final consonant they are written iê, uô, ươ and sound the same. So bia and biết share the same first half, and mua and muốn do too. In Saigon you will often hear them shrink before i, u, m, p: người like ngừi, hiểu like hỉu.

Take the minimal sets slowly: three words, three lengths. If mắt comes out like mát, you are lengthening ă; if mắt and mất merge, your â is too open.

== Initial consonants

This is where Saigon and Hanoi differ most, and where your Mandarin does the most work.

| spelling | Saigon sound | Mandarin or German anchor |
|---|---|---|
| d, gi | y as in "yes" | Mandarin 一 onset (yī) |
| đ | a real d, with a slight inward gulp | German d is accepted |
| v | v | German w |
| r | tongue curled back, close to an English r | Mandarin r in 日 |
| tr | curled-back t, like Mandarin zh | 知 |
| ch | flat t-plus-sh, like Mandarin j | 機 |
| s | curled-back s, like Mandarin sh | 是 |
| x | plain s | Mandarin s, 四 |
| t | unaspirated t | Mandarin d, 大 |
| th | aspirated t | Mandarin t, 他 |
| c, k | unaspirated k | Mandarin g, 個 |
| kh | aspirated k (Hanoi adds friction, like the ch in "Bach") | Mandarin k, 卡 |
| qu | kw: careful Saigon keeps the k, quá and not wá | Mandarin 瓜 (guā) |
| ng, ngh | the ng of "sing", at the start | German "Angst" without the A |
| nh | ñ | Spanish "señor" |
| ph | f | Mandarin f |
| g, gh | soft g with friction | Spanish "amigo" |
| b | b with a slight inward gulp | German b is accepted |

The two big habits to fight are German ones. First, German t, p, k are aspirated at the start of a word; Vietnamese t, c are not, and th, kh are. Use your Mandarin pairs d/t and g/k, they map exactly. Second, German has no ng at the beginning of a syllable. Say "singen", stop after "sing", then start "ngon" from that position.

### Saigon versus the written form

d, gi and r are three spellings that Hanoi reads as one sound (z). Saigon reads d and gi as y and keeps r apart. That is why a Saigon speaker hears the difference between ra and da, and why you should too.

The minimal pairs are for your ear as much as your mouth. When you play one on these pages, listen three times before you say it.

== Careful Saigon, fast Saigon, Hanoi

Three things get mixed up under the word "correct", so hold them apart.

The first is care. Inside Saigon there is a careful form and a fast form of the same accent. Careful keeps the k in qu (quá, not wá), keeps v as v (về, not yề), keeps s apart from x and tr apart from ch. Fast speech drops all four. Careful Saigon is what you produce, because it is never wrong in any situation, and because dropping a distinction later costs nothing while adding one costs months.

The second is dialect. d and gi are y in the South and z in the North. r is curled back in the South and z in the North. The five tones with hỏi and ngã falling together are Southern, and so are the finals: một sounds like mộc, and tinh loses its nh but keeps a trace of it in the vowel. None of this is carelessness and none of it gets "corrected". You never produce the Northern forms; they are named in the notes so that you understand a Hanoi speaker, nothing more.

The trap sits where the two meet. qu with its k sounds the same in careful Saigon and in Hanoi, so someone might tell you that the careful form is the Northern one. It is not, it is simply the careful one. But if a correction ever asks you to say dạ as zạ, or to split hỏi from ngã, that is not care any more, that is the Hanoi accent, and you decline it: your goal is to sound like a Saigon speaker in their twenties, not like a news reader from the North.

== Final consonants

Neither Mandarin nor German prepares you for Vietnamese finals. Mandarin has only n and ng at the end; German has every consonant but releases them with a puff. Vietnamese has eight finals and never releases the stops.

| final | how | Saigon |
|---|---|---|
| -m | close the lips, hum | same as written |
| -n | tongue at the teeth | after every vowel except i and ê (iê follows the rule: tiền like tiềng) it becomes -ng |
| -ng | back of the tongue | after o, ô, u as the whole vowel the lips close at the same moment: "ngm" (not after uô: uống) |
| -nh | the nh of nhà, at the end | after a: anh is [ăn], short a and n at the teeth; after i and ê: n, and the vowel pulls back toward ư or ơ |
| -p, -t, -c, -ch | stop, hold, no release, no puff | the syllable ends in silence |
| -t | tongue at the teeth, no release | after every vowel except i and ê (iê follows the rule: tiết like tiếc) it becomes -c |
| -c | back of the tongue, no release | after o, ô, u the lips close too: "kp" |
| -ch | a stopped nh | after a: sách is [sắt], short a and a cut t; after i and ê: a cut t, the vowel pulls back the same way |

### What sounds the same, and what only nearly does

These pairs really are the same in Saigon, and your tandems will say them that way:

- ăn = ăng, bàn = bàng, tiền = tiềng (-n becomes -ng)
- mắt = mắc, một = mộc, tiết = tiếc (-t becomes -c)

These are not the same, although fast speech and the voice flatten them:

- tin and tinh, chín and chính, xin and xinh. The final is n in both, but tinh pulls the vowel back toward ư, so it sounds like tưn, and chính like chứn. Two words, kept apart in careful speech.
- bệnh, kênh, ếch: after ê the vowel pulls toward ơ, so bệnh is close to bợn.

anh ends in a real n at the teeth, [ăn]; ăn ends in ng, [ăng]. They differ only in the final, the other way round from what the spelling suggests. sách ends in a cut t, [sắt]; sát itself ends in c, [sác]. This is why your spelling will be learned from reading, not from hearing.

### Stopped syllables and tone

A syllable ending in p, t, c, ch can only carry sắc or nặng: short high, or short low. Listen to học, tốt, đẹp, sách: two are high, two are low, all four are cut. If a final sounds like a German word ending, with a small puff after the t, cut the air off with the tongue or lips and leave it there.

### Numbers

One to ten as a row, because they are a finals drill in disguise: một (t said as c, lips close), bốn (n said as ng, lips close), năm and tám (m), chín (n at the teeth, clear i, not chính), mười (full glide in careful speech; you will hear mừi). Say them as a chain until they run.

== Who you are, in Saigon Vietnamese

The sounds now carry content: the self-introduction you send to every tandem, plus the Saigon contractions and particles that make it sound like Saigon rather than a textbook.

### The persona sentences

Seven sentences, and they answer the first seven questions every Vietnamese person asks. Learn them as one block.

Note ba and má: that is how the South says dad and mom. Hanoi says bố and mẹ; you will read those, never say them.

### Saigon contractions

anh ấy becomes ảnh, cô ấy becomes cổ, ông ấy becomes ổng, bà ấy becomes bả, chị ấy becomes chỉ. The pair becomes one syllable with a hỏi tone. In casual Saigon speech everyone says these; the full forms belong to polite or formal speech. About your own parents ổng and bả sound casual: fine with friends, cheeky in front of older people.

### Particles

- hả at the end turns a statement into a checking question, like 是喔 or 嗎 with a raised eyebrow.
- nha softens a request or a statement, close to 喔 or 吧. It is soft and short; do not stress it.
- nghen is the warmer, more familiar nha. It sounds older or Mekong; it fits your father, while tandems in their twenties write nha.
- Thiệt hả? and Vậy hả? are the two reactions you will hear most in a chat. Thiệt is the Southern form of thật.

### Mandarin bridge

Anh là người Đức = 我是德國人, but inside người Đức the order flips: person first, then German. Anh nói được tiếng Trung: được after the verb is like 得 in 聽得懂, or simply 會, "can, manage to". Trung Quốc is 中國 read in Hán Việt: 中 = trung, 國 = quốc.

== How a Vietnamese sentence works

The good news first: Vietnamese grammar is close to Mandarin. Words never change. There is no conjugation, no plural ending, no article, and no tense inside the verb. The order is subject, verb, object, as in 我學越南文.

### No 是 before an adjective

An adjective is the predicate on its own, as in 我很累. là only links two nouns, like 是 between nouns.

### What describes a noun comes after it

This is the biggest difference from Mandarin. 德國人 is người Đức (person, German), 這個瓶子 is cái chai này (thing, bottle, this), 德文老師 is giáo viên tiếng Đức (teacher, German), 我爸 is ba anh (dad, me).

### Pronouns are family words

You do not say I and you; you say what you are to each other. To someone younger you are anh and they are em. To your father you are con and he is ba. Even thanks names the person.

### Negation and yes-or-no questions

không before the verb is 不 or 沒. At the end of a sentence it turns it into a yes-or-no question, like 嗎. Question words stay where the answer goes, as in Mandarin.

### Already and not yet: rồi and chưa

rồi at the end of the verb phrase is 了, already. chưa at the end asks whether it has happened yet, 了嗎; before the verb it means 還沒.

### How much: rất, quá, lắm

rất goes before the adjective (很). quá and lắm go after it: quá in exclamations (好…喔、太), lắm for really, very.

### Small words around the verb

được after the verb means can (會、能). lại after the verb means again (再), tiếp means go on (繼續). chút after an adjective means a bit (一點). mới before the verb means just (剛).

### Ordering

Cho anh + number + classifier + thing: give me…. The classifier stands between the number and the noun, as in 一杯咖啡: ly for a glass, tô for a big bowl.

== Four places where you beat the voice

The voice is a model, not a person from Saigon, and it gets four sounds wrong. In the app every syllable with r, tr, s or a palatal final after i or ê is dotted; tap it to see what you say instead.

One thing first: many people in Taiwan say zh, ch and sh flat. For tr and s use the textbook, curled-back Mandarin of 知 and 是, or your Mandarin will merge them too.

1. **r** comes out as a z. You say it curled back like the Mandarin 日: ra, not za.
@@play s0049

2. **tr and ch** sound the same in the voice. You keep them apart: tr curled back like 知, ch flat like 機. con trai and cái chai are two different things.
@@play s0051

3. **s and x** sound the same in the voice. You keep them apart: s curled back like 是, x a plain s like 四.
@@play s0054

4. **chín and chính** sound the same in the voice. The final n really is the same in both, but the vowel is not: chín has a clear i, chính pulls toward ư. The same with tin and tinh, and after ê the trace goes toward ơ.

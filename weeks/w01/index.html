Week 1, day 1: the five Saigon tones, greetings

What today is for

Vietnamese spelling shows six tones. Saigon has five, because hỏi (ả) and ngã (ã) are pronounced the same. Your Mandarin gives you the categories; today is about calibrating the contours, which are not the Mandarin ones.

tonemarkSaigon contourMandarin comparison
ngangnonemid, flatlike tone 1 but lower, 33 rather than 55
huyềnàlow, falling gently, relaxed and slightly breathynot tone 4: it starts low and falls softly, no force
sắcárisingtone 2, nearly identical; on syllables ending in p, t, c, ch it is short and high
hỏi / ngãả, ãdips, then rises, longer than the othersa full tone 3 (214), the one you use in isolation
nặngạlow, short, cut off; on open syllables a tiny rise at the very endno Mandarin equivalent; think of a tone 3 that never gets to rise

What to listen for in the track: how short nặng is, how long hỏi is, and that huyền has no energy in it.

If the voice makes a break in the middle of ngã words (cũng, nữa, mã), that is the Northern realisation. Ignore it and say them exactly like the hỏi words.

Pronouns today

anh = I (you, a man, to someone younger) and also you (when they address you). em = you (to someone younger) and I (when they speak). The tandems call you anh and themselves em; you do the reverse. That is the whole system for this week. tôi appears on day 3 as the neutral I; mình, bạn and the rest come in week 2.

Shadowing hints

Greetings are said fast and low in Saigon. Chào em is almost one syllable long. Dạ is not "yes" in the German sense; it is what you say to show you are listening politely, and it starts almost every answer to someone you want to be polite to.

Week 1, day 2: vowels

What today is for

Twelve vowel letters (i and y share a sound), six of them with a hook or hat that changes the sound entirely. German gives you most of the qualities; the danger is length. Vietnamese a is long, ă and â are short, and length is not decorative: mắt, mất, mát are three words.

letterssoundnearest German or Mandarin sound
along, openBahn
ăsame quality, very short, clippedthe a in "hat", cut even shorter
âshort, central, mouth half closedthe e in "bitte", or Mandarin 恩 without the n
eopenBett
êclosedBeet, Mandarin 欸
i, ysame sound, spelling onlyMandarin 一
oopen, roundedoffen
ôclosed, roundedOfen, Mandarin 喔
ơunrounded ô, mouth relaxedEnglish "bird" without the r, shorter; Mandarin 婀 in 婀娜
uclosed, roundedMandarin 烏
ưu with the lips spread as for ino clean match in Mandarin or German; start from u and unround

Glides

ia, ua, ưa at the end of a syllable are i, u, ư gliding into a lazy ơ. Before a final consonant they are written iê, uô, ươ and sound the same. So bia and biết share the same first half, and mua and muốn do too.

Saigon note

After o, ô, u a final ng or c closes the lips at the end (không, học), and một belongs there too because Saigon says its -t as -c. Day 4 drills that; today just notice it in một.

Shadowing hints

Take the minimal sets slowly: three words, three lengths. Then the phrases at speed. If mất and mắt come out the same, you are lengthening ă; keep it as short as the voice does.

Week 1, day 3: initial consonants, the Saigon way

What today is for

This is where Saigon and Hanoi differ most, and where your Mandarin does the most work.

spellingSaigon soundMandarin or German anchor
d, giy as in "yes"Mandarin 一 onset (yī)
đa real d, with a slight inward gulpGerman d is accepted
vvGerman w
rtongue curled back, close to an English rMandarin r in 日 is close
trcurled-back t, like Mandarin zh知
chflat t-plus-sh, like Mandarin j機
scurled-back s, like Mandarin sh (many young speakers say plain s)是
xplain sMandarin s, 四
tunaspirated tMandarin d, 大
thaspirated tMandarin t, 他
c, k, qunaspirated kMandarin g, 個
khaspirated k with frictionMandarin k or h, German ch in "Bach"
quwMandarin 我 onset
ng, nghthe ng of "sing", at the startno Mandarin help; German "Angst" without the A
nhñSpanish "señor", the ny in "canyon"

The two big habits to fight are German ones. First, German t, p, k are aspirated at the start of a word; Vietnamese t, c are not, and Vietnamese th, kh are. Use your Mandarin pairs d/t and g/k, they map exactly. Second, German has no ng at the beginning of a syllable. Say "singen", stop after "sing", then start "ngon" from that position.

Saigon versus the written form

d, gi and r are three spellings that Hanoi reads as one sound (z). Saigon reads d and gi as y and keeps r apart. That is why a Saigon speaker hears the difference between ra and da, and why you should too.

tr and ch, s and x: Saigon keeps both pairs apart in careful speech. Your tandems in their twenties may or may not; do what the voice does.

Shadowing hints

The minimal pairs are for your ear as much as your mouth. Listen to each pair three times before you say it. On the phrases, the particle nha at the end is soft and short; do not stress it.

Week 1, day 4: final consonants and the Saigon mergers

What today is for

Neither Mandarin nor German prepares you for Vietnamese finals. Mandarin has only n and ng at the end; German has every consonant but releases them with a puff. Vietnamese has eight finals and never releases the stops.

finalhownote

The mergers you must have

These pairs are homophones in Saigon, and the tandems will say them that way:

  • tin = tinh, chín = chính, xin = xinh (front vowel i: -n and -nh fall together)
  • ăn = ăng, bàn = bàng, tiền = tiềng (other vowels: -n becomes -ng)
  • mắt = mắc, một = mộc, tiết = tiếc (-t becomes -c)
  • anh ends in a real n at the teeth, [ăn]; ăn ends in ng, [ăng]. They differ only in the final, and the other way round from what the spelling suggests. sách ends in a cut t, [sát]; sát itself ends in c, [sác]

This is why a Northerner sometimes cannot tell what a Saigon person wrote from what they said, and why your spelling will be learned from reading, not from hearing.

Stopped syllables and tone

A syllable ending in p, t, c, ch can only carry sắc or nặng. Short high, or short low. Listen to học, tốt, đẹp, sách: two are high, two are low, all four are cut.

Numbers

One to ten as a row, because they are a finals drill in disguise: một (t said as c, lips close), bốn (n said as ng, lips close), năm and tám (m), chín (soft n), mười (long glide). Say them as a chain until they run.

Shadowing hints

If a final sounds like a German word ending (with a small puff after the t), it is wrong. Cut the air off with the tongue or lips and leave it there. Recording yourself once today is worth doing: the puff is very audible on playback and almost invisible while you speak.

What today is for

The first four days were sound. Today the sounds carry content: the self-introduction you will send to every tandem, plus the small set of Saigon contractions and particles that make it sound like Saigon rather than a textbook. Half of today's track is sentences you already know from days 1 to 4, now in the order of a real first conversation.

The persona sentences

Anh là người Đức. Anh là giáo viên tiếng Đức. Anh dạy tiếng Đức cho người Trung Quốc. Ba anh là người Việt. Má anh là người Trung Quốc. Anh nói được tiếng Trung. Anh mới học tiếng Việt.

Seven sentences, and they answer the first seven questions every Vietnamese person asks. Learn them as one block; by the end of the day they should come out without thinking.

Note ba and má: that is how the South says dad and mom. Hanoi says bố and mẹ; you will read those, never say them.

Saigon contractions

anh ấy becomes ảnh, cô ấy becomes cổ, ông ấy becomes ổng, bà ấy becomes bả, chị ấy becomes chỉ. The tone of the second word (ấy, sắc) folds into the first as a hỏi tone. Everyone in Saigon says these; the full forms sound like reading aloud.

Particles

  • hả at the end turns a statement into a checking question: Em ở Sài Gòn hả? Like 是喔 or 嗎 with a raised eyebrow.
  • nha softens a request or a statement: Em nói chậm chút nha. Mai nói tiếp nha. Close to 喔 or 吧.
  • nghen is the warmer, more familiar nha: Ngủ ngon nghen.
  • Thiệt hả? and Vậy hả? are the two reactions you will hear most in a chat. Thiệt is the Southern form of thật.

Mandarin bridge

Anh là người Đức = 我是德國人, word for word, including the topic-first shape. Anh nói được tiếng Trung: được after the verb is 得 in 說得 / 會, "manage to". Người Trung Quốc: 中國 read in Hán Việt is Trung Quốc; you now own your first two 漢字 correspondences, 中 = trung and 國 = quốc.

Today's output block

Send the seven persona sentences to one tandem as a message, in Vietnamese, with Em ở Sài Gòn hả? at the end. Ask them to correct anything. Their answer is your first piece of real Saigon input; save it, we use it in week 2.
